# Quadratic Functions

Quadratic functions take the shape of a parabola. Parabolas are utilised in bridges, radio telescopes, and car headlights, among other applications, due to their special shape properties. In fact, many famous landmarks are parabolas - for example, the Sydney Harbour Bridge.

![|425x191](https://mathspace-au-production-media.mathspace.co/media/upload/2020/functions/harbour_bridge_parabolas.svg)

## Identifying Key Features

Quadratic functions can be written in any of the following forms:

> [!NOTE] Forms of Quadratics
> General Form: $y=ax^{2}+bx+c$
> Factored or x-intercept form: $y=a(x-\alpha)(x-\beta)$
> Turning point form: $y=a(x-h)^{2}+k$
Each form has an advantage for different key features that can be identified quickly. However, for each form the role of $a$ remains the same.

That is:

- If $a < 0$ then the quadratic is concave down
- If $a > 0$ then the quadratic is concave up
- The larger the magnitude of $a$, the narrower the curve

## Intercepts

> [!NOTE] Intercepts
> The $x$-intercepts are where the parabola crosses the $x$-axis. This occurs when $y=0$.
The $y$-intercept is where the parabola crosses the $y$-axis. This occurs when $x=0$.

Parabolas can have none, one, or two $x$-intercepts as shown below.

## Maximum and Minimum  Values
Maximum or minimum values are also known as the turning points, and they are found at the vertex of the parabola.
![|256x258](https://mathspace-au-production-media.mathspace.co/media/upload/images/10-functions-and-logs/minimum-values.svg)
Parabolas that are concave up have a minimum value. This means the $y$-value will never go under a certain value.
