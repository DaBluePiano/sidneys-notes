# Draft 2

When I turn on the news at night, it feels like the same story over and over again—bombings, refugees, soldiers in trenches. Whole towns flattened, families gone, lives ended in a split second. If this is what war looks like, then how can anyone honestly say it’s justifiable?

Wilfred Owen answers that question best in his poem *Dulce et Decorum Est*. He doesn’t show war as brave or heroic, but as something disgusting and cruel. His image of a soldier choking on poison gas—“obscene as cancer, as bitter as the cud”—sticks with you because it shows war for what it really is: ugly. He was writing back to Jessie Pope, who had made war sound like a fun adventure. Owen makes it clear—it’s not. People suffer and die in terrible ways, and that can’t be justified.

People sometimes argue war is for peace, but is that really true? Even if leaders sign treaties, the damage doesn’t just disappear. Families are left grieving forever. Soldiers’ loved ones sit by the phone waiting for news, knowing that one call could change everything. Peace written on paper doesn’t erase pain or bring the dead back. It just hides the suffering underneath.

And it’s not only about leaders. Sure, politicians start wars, but ordinary people let them go on too. We sit behind screens, watch it like entertainment, and then carry on with our day. But for the people living through it, there’s no “off switch.” If we think war isn’t justifiable, then we need to stop being passive about it and push for other ways—diplomacy, compromise, literally anything but war.

War also wrecks countries completely. A bomb doesn’t just hit a target—it takes down hospitals, schools, roads, homes. Things built over decades gone in minutes. And unlike a game, you don’t just restart. Rebuilding takes years, sometimes generations, and all that money and time could’ve gone into making lives better instead of destroying them.

So no, war is not justifiable. It doesn’t bring peace, it brings pain. It sacrifices people for politics and leaves scars that never really heal. Yes, conflicts will always exist, but killing thousands of people can’t be the answer. There are always other ways forward—we just have to care enough to find them.
